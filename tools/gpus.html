Reading the table

Bandwidth decides speed, not TFLOPS.

  • What the LLM score means A transparent heuristic, not gospel: bandwidth 45%, VRAM 30%, dense compute 25%, each normalized to the fastest card here. It's weighted for serving — decode is memory-bound — so a card wins by feeding data fast and holding a lot of it. Re-sort any column to rank by that metric alone.
  • VRAM sets what fits The first gate. A model that doesn't fit doesn't run — no amount of compute helps. This is why 24 GB consumer cards define the consumer ceiling.
  • Memory bandwidth sets token speed LLM decode is memory-bound, not compute-bound. Tokens-per-second tracks bandwidth far more than TFLOPS — an H100 SXM's real edge over a 4090 is its ~3.3 TB/s, not its FLOPS.
  • Low precision is a generational divide FP8 (Ada/Hopper) and FP4 (Blackwell) roughly double effective throughput for models that support them. Ampere (3090, A100) tops out at INT8 — a real reason the 4090 punches above the 3090 beyond raw specs.
  • Watch the power column On a multi-GPU rig, TDP × card count is your PSU and cooling budget. Thirty-two 350 W cards is 11.2 kW before the rest of the machine — which is the whole reason to care where the power comes from.
  • Beyond NVIDIA — real, with caveats vLLM officially lists AMD Instinct MI200/MI300/MI350 and Radeon RX 7900/9000 as ROCm targets — the MI300X (192 GB) and MI355X (288 GB) genuinely lead on memory-per-card. AMD's new 32 GB workstation cards (Radeon AI PRO R9700, PRO W7800) put serious VRAM on a desktop. Intel Arc runs through vLLM's XPU path but it's rougher — no pre-built wheels, Python 3.12 only — and Gaudi needs its own software stack. CUDA is still the smoothest road; the others cost you setup time.
